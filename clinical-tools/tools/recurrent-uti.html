Evidence-based management pathway for recurrent urinary tract infections

Definition of Recurrent UTI

Recurrent UTI (rUTI) Definition:

≥2 infections in 6 months OR ≥3 infections in 12 months

Classification

Reinfection (90-95%) New infection with different organism or same organism after complete eradication
Relapse (5-10%) Same organism within 2 weeks of treatment completion, suggests persistent focus

Clinical Presentation

Typical symptoms include:

  • Dysuria (painful urination)
  • Frequency and urgency
  • Suprapubic pain or discomfort
  • Haematuria (visible or non-visible)
  • New onset or worsening urinary incontinence
  • Cloudy or foul-smelling urine
⚠️ Red Flag Symptoms (Require Urgent Assessment):
  • Fever >38°C with rigors (suggests pyelonephritis)
  • Loin pain or tenderness
  • Sepsis or systemic unwellness
  • Visible haematuria in older patients (>50 years)
  • Acute urinary retention
  • New neurological symptoms
🔍

Initial Assessment

History Taking

Infection Pattern Frequency, temporal relationship to activities (sexual activity, menstruation), previous treatments and responses
Urological History Previous urinary tract surgery, stones, catheterization, voiding dysfunction, incontinence
Gynaecological History Menopausal status, vaginal atrophy symptoms, use of spermicides/diaphragm, vaginal discharge
Medical Comorbidities Diabetes, immunosuppression, neurological conditions, renal disease, previous pelvic radiation
Medication Review Antibiotic history, resistance patterns, immunosuppressants, anticholinergics
Fluid and Hygiene Habits Daily fluid intake, voiding frequency, bowel habits, personal hygiene practices

Physical Examination

General Examination
  • Temperature and vital signs
  • Abdominal examination (suprapubic tenderness, palpable bladder)
  • Loin tenderness (renal angle)
  • Peripheral oedema
Female-Specific
  • External genitalia inspection
  • Vaginal examination (atrophy, prolapse)
  • Pelvic organ prolapse assessment
Male-Specific Examination:
  • Digital rectal examination (prostate assessment)
  • External genitalia examination (phimosis, meatal stenosis)
  • Testicular examination if scrotal symptoms

Diagnostic Investigations

First-Line Investigations (Primary Care)

Investigation Indication Interpretation
Urine Dipstick Initial assessment during symptomatic episode Positive nitrites/leucocytes suggests UTI. Negative dipstick in symptomatic patient does not exclude UTI
Urine MC&S All rUTI patients; send before starting antibiotics if possible Confirms diagnosis, identifies organism and sensitivities. Consider significant if ≥10³ CFU/mL in symptomatic patient
Post-void Residual (PVR) All patients with rUTI Bladder scan or catheterization. >100mL abnormal, >200mL significant. Suggests incomplete bladder emptying
Renal Function (U&E) Baseline assessment, elderly, diabetics, suspected complications Assess renal function, guide antibiotic choice and dosing
HbA1c / Blood Glucose If diabetes suspected or risk factors present Uncontrolled diabetes increases UTI risk

Specialist Investigations (Secondary Care)

Investigation Indication Key Findings
Renal Tract Ultrasound First-line imaging for rUTI, haematuria, suspected obstruction Hydronephrosis, stones, bladder abnormalities, PVR confirmation, renal masses
Flexible Cystoscopy Persistent haematuria, >50yrs with rUTI, suspected bladder pathology Bladder tumours, stones, foreign bodies, urethral stricture, interstitial cystitis
Flow Rate & Voiding Study Suspected voiding dysfunction, high PVR Qmax <15 mL/s abnormal, obstructive pattern, flow curve analysis
CT Urogram (CTU) Unexplained haematuria >50yrs, suspected stones, complex anatomy Detailed upper tract imaging, stone disease, structural abnormalities, masses
Urodynamic Studies Neurogenic bladder, complex voiding dysfunction, pre-surgery assessment Detrusor overactivity, bladder compliance, voiding pressure, obstruction
MRI Pelvis Suspected fistula, complex pelvic anatomy, soft tissue assessment Vesicovaginal/rectovesical fistula, pelvic floor dysfunction
⚠️ Mandatory Investigations Before Referral:
  • At least 2 MSU cultures with sensitivities during symptomatic episodes
  • Post-void residual measurement
  • Renal function (U&E)
  • Renal tract ultrasound (can be arranged in primary care or by urology)

Acute Management of rUTI Episodes

Treatment Principles

✓ Key Management Principles:
  • Send MSU for culture BEFORE starting antibiotics when possible
  • Use culture-directed therapy based on previous sensitivities
  • Avoid unnecessary antibiotic exposure to reduce resistance
  • Consider delayed prescribing for mild symptoms in low-risk patients
  • Shorter courses (3 days) as effective as longer courses for uncomplicated lower UTI
  • Review and adjust antibiotics based on culture results

First-Line Antibiotic Treatment (Community)

Based on PHE/NICE guidance and local antimicrobial guidelines - always check local formulary

1. Nitrofurantoin (if eGFR >45 mL/min)

100mg modified-release BD for 3 days

Advantages: Low resistance rates, minimal effect on gut flora, good bladder concentration

Contraindications: eGFR <45, G6PD deficiency, third trimester pregnancy, avoid in pyelonephritis

2. Trimethoprim (if low resistance risk)

200mg BD for 3 days

Advantages: Well-tolerated, inexpensive

Resistance concerns: Increasing resistance (30-40% in some areas) - check local data

3. Pivmecillinam

400mg initial dose, then 200mg TDS for 3 days

Advantages: Low resistance, good alternative when others contraindicated

Note: Take with food, may cause GI upset

Second-Line Options (If First-Line Unsuitable)

Fosfomycin

3g single dose (sachet dissolved in water)

Indication: Uncomplicated lower UTI, resistant organisms, patient preference for single dose

Advantages: Single-dose treatment, broad spectrum, low resistance

Cost: More expensive, reserve for appropriate cases

Cefalexin

500mg BD/TDS for 3-5 days

Indication: When first-line unsuitable, pregnancy (caution with other options)

Note: Broader spectrum, consider resistance development

Pyelonephritis Treatment (Upper UTI)

⚠️ Criteria for Pyelonephritis: Fever >38°C, loin pain/tenderness, systemically unwell, nausea/vomiting

Mild-Moderate Pyelonephritis (Oral Treatment)

Cefalexin 500mg TDS for 7-10 days

Alternative: Co-amoxiclav 500/125mg TDS for 7-10 days (if sensitivities known)

If penicillin allergy: Ciprofloxacin 500mg BD for 7 days OR Trimethoprim 200mg BD for 14 days (check sensitivities)

🏥 Hospital Admission Criteria:
  • Sepsis or severe systemic upset (SIRS criteria)
  • Unable to tolerate oral antibiotics (vomiting)
  • Pregnancy
  • Immunocompromised
  • Suspected obstruction or renal abscess
  • Single functioning kidney
  • Lack of response to oral treatment within 24-48 hours

Special Populations

Pregnancy
  • Asymptomatic bacteriuria: Requires treatment
  • First-line: Cefalexin 500mg BD 7 days
  • Avoid: Trimethoprim (first trimester), nitrofurantoin (third trimester), quinolones
  • Low threshold for IV treatment and admission
Elderly/Catheterized
  • Do NOT treat asymptomatic bacteriuria
  • Only treat if symptomatic
  • Consider catheter change if long-term catheter in situ
  • Review need for catheter

Treatment Failure

No improvement after 48 hours of appropriate antibiotic Send repeat MSU, review antibiotic sensitivities, consider compliance issues, reassess for complications (abscess, obstruction), consider imaging
🛡️

Antibiotic Prophylaxis for rUTI

Indications for Antibiotic Prophylaxis (EAU/NICE):
  • Frequent rUTI (≥3 infections in 12 months) significantly impacting quality of life
  • Failed non-antibiotic preventive measures
  • No correctable underlying structural abnormality
  • Discussed benefits/risks with patient including antimicrobial resistance

Prophylaxis Options

1. Nitrofurantoin (First-Line)

50-100mg once daily at night

Duration: 3-6 month trial initially, review regularly

Advantages: Low resistance rates, minimal impact on bowel flora, well-tolerated

Monitoring: eGFR at baseline and 3-monthly (contraindicated if eGFR <45)

Side effects: Nausea, peripheral neuropathy (rare, long-term use), pulmonary reactions (rare)

2. Trimethoprim

100mg once daily at night

Duration: 3-6 month trial initially

Advantages: Well-tolerated, inexpensive

Concerns: Higher resistance rates (30-40%), avoid in pregnancy (first trimester)

Note: Check local resistance patterns before prescribing

3. Cefalexin

125mg once daily at night

Indication: When nitrofurantoin/trimethoprim unsuitable or ineffective

Advantages: Generally well-tolerated

Concerns: Broader spectrum, potential for C. difficile, ESBL development

Post-Coital Prophylaxis

✓ For Women with UTI Triggered by Sexual Activity:

Single dose within 2 hours after intercourse:

  • Nitrofurantoin 100mg (preferred)
  • Trimethoprim 100mg (alternative)
  • Cefalexin 250mg (if others unsuitable)

More targeted approach, lower antibiotic exposure than continuous prophylaxis

Patient Self-Treatment Strategy

Suitable for motivated, reliable patients with established pattern:
  • Provide prescription for 3-day course to keep at home
  • Patient recognizes typical UTI symptoms early
  • Instructions to send MSU before starting antibiotics
  • Clear criteria for when to seek medical review
  • Regular follow-up to review effectiveness and frequency

Antibiotics to provide: Nitrofurantoin 100mg MR BD or Trimethoprim 200mg BD (based on sensitivities)

Monitoring on Prophylaxis

Parameter Frequency Action Required
Clinical review 3 months, then 6-monthly Assess efficacy, side effects, quality of life impact
UTI frequency Each review Document breakthrough infections and organisms
Renal function (eGFR) Baseline, 3-monthly if on nitrofurantoin Stop nitrofurantoin if eGFR <45
LFTs If symptoms develop (rare) Assess for drug-induced hepatitis

When to Stop Prophylaxis

Trial off treatment after 6-12 months If rUTI do not recur, prophylaxis can be discontinued. If recurrence occurs, consider restarting for further 6-12 months
Breakthrough infections on prophylaxis Send MSU, review antibiotic choice, check compliance, investigate for underlying pathology
Side effects or contraindications develop Consider alternative prophylactic agent or non-antibiotic strategies
⚠️ Important Counseling Points:
  • Prophylaxis reduces UTI frequency by ~50-90% (varies by study)
  • Not a cure - infections may still occur
  • Risk of antimicrobial resistance to patient and community
  • Breakthrough infections may be resistant to prophylactic antibiotic
  • Should be used alongside non-antibiotic preventive measures
  • Regular review essential - not lifelong treatment by default

Non-Antibiotic Preventive Measures

✓ First-Line Prevention - Should be tried BEFORE antibiotic prophylaxis

All patients with rUTI should receive advice on behavioural and lifestyle modifications

Evidence-Based Lifestyle Modifications

1. Adequate Fluid Intake Target 1.5-2 litres daily (unless fluid restriction required). Increases urinary flow, reduces bacterial concentration, improves bladder emptying. Water is optimal - avoid excessive caffeine and alcohol
2. Complete Bladder Emptying Avoid delaying micturition when urge occurs. Allow adequate time for complete voiding. Double voiding technique if high PVR identified. Consider timed voiding schedule (every 3-4 hours)
3. Post-Coital Voiding (Women) Evidence: Moderate quality evidence supporting benefit. Urinate within 15 minutes after sexual intercourse to flush bacteria from urethra. Simple, cost-free intervention
4. Avoid Spermicides and Diaphragms Spermicides alter vaginal flora and increase UTI risk. Consider alternative contraception methods if rUTI problem
5. Manage Constipation Chronic constipation associated with recurrent UTI, particularly in children and elderly. Adequate fibre intake, laxatives if needed. Bowel dysfunction can affect bladder emptying
6. Perineal Hygiene Front-to-back wiping after defecation. Avoid harsh soaps, douches, or vaginal deodorants. Cotton underwear, avoid tight clothing

Cranberry Products

Evidence Summary (EAU/Cochrane):

Mechanism: Proanthocyanidins (PACs) may prevent bacterial adhesion to uroepithelium

Evidence Grade: Moderate quality evidence showing modest benefit

Efficacy: May reduce UTI recurrence by approximately 26-35% in women with rUTI

Recommended Products:
  • Cranberry capsules/tablets: 36mg PAC daily (standardized extract preferred)
  • Cranberry juice: 240ml twice daily (must be unsweetened/low sugar - commercial juices often inadequate)
  • Duration: Minimum 3-6 months trial to assess effectiveness

Contraindications: Warfarin interaction (potential INR increase), kidney stones (oxalate content), diabetes (if sweetened products)

NICE: Can be considered but evidence less robust than antibiotic prophylaxis

D-Mannose

Evidence Summary:

Mechanism: Simple sugar that may prevent E. coli adhesion to bladder wall

Evidence Grade: Weak. The MERIT trial (2024) found no benefit over placebo for preventing recurrent UTI; earlier small RCTs were inconsistent

Dose: 2g daily (powder dissolved in water) or 500mg tablets twice daily

Duration: 3-6 month trial

Studies suggest: Not recommended as an effective prophylaxis strategy on current evidence

Safety: Generally well-tolerated, minimal side effects. Caution in diabetes (affects blood sugar)

Not recommended in NICE; EAU gives only a weak recommendation. Shared decision-making if the patient wishes to try it.

Probiotics

Evidence Summary:

Mechanism: Lactobacillus species may restore vaginal flora, compete with uropathogens

Evidence Grade: Conflicting evidence, quality of studies variable

Recommended Strains:
  • Lactobacillus crispatus
  • Lactobacillus rhamnosus GR-1
  • Lactobacillus reuteri RC-14

Route: Oral or vaginal administration

Dose: 10⁸-10⁹ CFU daily

Current consensus: May be beneficial but insufficient evidence for routine recommendation. Consider as adjunct therapy

Vaginal Oestrogen (Postmenopausal Women)

✓ Strong Evidence Supporting Use

Indication: Postmenopausal women with rUTI and vaginal atrophy

Mechanism: Restores vaginal pH, improves vaginal epithelium, normalizes lactobacillus flora

Evidence: High-quality evidence showing ~50% reduction in rUTI

Prescribing Options:
  • Estriol cream 0.1%: 0.5g intravaginally daily for 2 weeks, then twice weekly maintenance
  • Estradiol pessaries 10mcg: 1 pessary daily for 2 weeks, then twice weekly
  • Estradiol vaginal ring: Replace every 3 months (continuous low-dose delivery)

Safety: Very low systemic absorption, minimal risks, no routine monitoring required

Contraindications: Breast cancer (discuss with oncology), undiagnosed vaginal bleeding, active VTE

NICE strongly recommends for postmenopausal women with rUTI

Methenamine Hippurate

Antiseptic Agent (Not an Antibiotic)

Mechanism: Converted to formaldehyde in acidic urine (pH <6), which has antibacterial properties

Dose: 1g twice daily (Hiprex®)

Evidence: Cochrane review shows modest benefit, particularly in patients without renal tract abnormalities

Enhancing efficacy: Vitamin C 500mg-1g daily may help acidify urine (aim pH <6)

Limitations: Less effective if urine pH >6 (check with urinalysis). Not effective if urea-splitting organisms present (Proteus)

Interactions: Avoid with sulfonamides, alkalinizing agents

Consider as alternative to antibiotic prophylaxis in selected patients

Other Herbal/Complementary Therapies

Uva-ursi (Bearberry) Insufficient evidence for routine recommendation. Contains arbutin (converted to hydroquinone). Safety concerns with long-term use
Vitamin C May acidify urine but evidence for UTI prevention weak. High doses may increase stone risk
Acupuncture Limited evidence. Small studies suggest possible benefit but need larger trials
Green Tea Extract In vitro antibacterial activity but no clinical trial evidence for rUTI prevention

Recommended Preventive Strategy

1 ALL patients: Lifestyle modifications (fluid intake, voiding habits, post-coital voiding if relevant)
2 Postmenopausal women: Add vaginal oestrogen if atrophy present
3 If still recurrent: Consider cranberry (36mg PAC) for a 3-6 month trial
4 If non-antibiotic measures fail: Consider antibiotic prophylaxis (see Prophylaxis tab)

Referral Pathway & Management Levels

Primary Care Management (Suitable for Community Treatment)

✓ Manage in Primary Care If:
  • Uncomplicated rUTI in otherwise healthy women
  • No structural abnormalities on imaging
  • Post-void residual <100mL
  • Good response to standard treatment
  • No concerning symptoms (haematuria resolved, no systemic features)
  • Patient willing to try non-antibiotic preventive measures

Primary Care Management Pathway

1 Initial Assessment & Investigation

History, examination, MSU culture x2 (symptomatic episodes), post-void residual, renal function, consider renal ultrasound

2 Lifestyle & Behavioural Advice

Fluid intake, voiding habits, post-coital measures, contraception review, perineal hygiene

3 Non-Antibiotic Prophylaxis Trial

Cranberry products, vaginal oestrogen (if postmenopausal), probiotics consideration. Trial for 3-6 months

4 Consider Antibiotic Prophylaxis

If non-antibiotic measures fail and significant impact on quality of life. Nitrofurantoin or trimethoprim. 3-6 month trial with regular review

5 Ongoing Monitoring

3-monthly reviews, document infection frequency, adjust strategy as needed, trial off prophylaxis after 6-12 months

Indications for Urology Referral (Routine)

🔄 Refer to Urology If:
  • Persistent rUTI despite appropriate prophylaxis (>3 infections/year on treatment)
  • Structural abnormality identified: Stones, hydronephrosis, bladder abnormality, suspected fistula
  • Elevated post-void residual (>100mL, particularly if >200mL)
  • Recurrent UTI in men (always warrant investigation for structural cause)
  • Suspected voiding dysfunction: Neurogenic bladder, obstructive symptoms, incontinence
  • Relapsing UTI (same organism <2 weeks after treatment - suggests persistent focus)
  • Unusual organisms or multi-resistant bacteria
  • Suspected interstitial cystitis / bladder pain syndrome
  • Patient declining/unable to tolerate antibiotic prophylaxis and seeking alternatives

Urgent/2-Week Wait Referral Criteria

⚠️ URGENT Referral Required (2WW Cancer Pathway):
  • Visible haematuria age ≥45 (especially if persistent or recurrent)
  • Visible haematuria age <45 with: Unexplained haematuria AND thrombocytosis, dysuria, or pelvic/rectal mass
  • Non-visible haematuria age ≥60 (after UTI excluded/treated)
  • Palpable renal mass
  • Pelvic mass (consider gynae if clearly gynae origin)

NICE NG12 - Suspected Cancer Recognition & Referral

Acute/Emergency Referral

🚨 Same-Day Assessment/Admission Required:
  • Suspected urosepsis (fever >38°C, rigors, hypotension, tachycardia, SIRS criteria)
  • Acute urinary retention (unable to pass urine)
  • Suspected pyonephrosis/obstructed infected kidney (fever + loin pain + imaging showing hydronephrosis)
  • Pyelonephritis unable to tolerate oral antibiotics
  • Pyelonephritis in pregnancy
  • Suspected renal/perinephric abscess
  • Single functioning kidney with pyelonephritis

Information to Include in Referral

Essential Information for Effective Referral:
  • Infection pattern: Frequency, duration, temporal patterns
  • Microbiology: At least 2 MSU culture results with organisms and sensitivities
  • Investigations completed: Renal ultrasound report, post-void residual, renal function
  • Treatments tried: Acute treatment responses, prophylaxis trials (duration, agents, outcomes)
  • Non-antibiotic measures: What has been advised and tried
  • Impact on quality of life: Work absence, sexual function, psychological impact
  • Relevant past history: Previous urological procedures, gynaecological history, comorbidities
  • Current medications: Including contraception, immunosuppressants

What to Expect from Urology Service

Outpatient Assessment
  • Detailed history and examination
  • Review of investigations
  • Urine flow rate ± post-void residual
  • Cystoscopy if indicated
  • Further imaging if required (CT, MRI)
Management Options
  • Identify and treat structural causes
  • Specialist prophylaxis strategies
  • Intravesical therapies (e.g., hyaluronic acid)
  • Surgical intervention if indicated
  • Multidisciplinary input (gynae, ID)

Special Situations

Recurrent UTI in Men ALWAYS refer for investigation. Higher likelihood of structural abnormality (prostate disease, stones, stricture, bladder outlet obstruction). Requires thorough urological assessment including flow rate, PVR, prostate examination, imaging, possible cystoscopy
Post-Surgical rUTI If rUTI following urological surgery (TURP, urethral surgery, stone treatment), refer back to operating surgeon or urology for assessment of surgical site complications
Pregnancy-Related Obstetric input essential. Urology referral if structural abnormalities or persistent issues. Lower threshold for admission and IV treatment
Immunocompromised Patients May need joint management with infectious diseases. Consider atypical organisms, fungal infections. Urology input for structural assessment
